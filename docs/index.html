UniWALM: Unified World Action Language Model for Embodied Robot Control
Authors: Rahul Sajnani, Diego Romeres, Mingyuan Zhang, Kai Gao, Carl Qi, Baichuan Huang, Srinath Sridhar, Dylan Randle, Lillian J. Ratliff, Jose Barreiros, Manikantan Nambi, Jeroen Vanbaar
First: 2026-10-20T17:59:04+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T17:59:04+00:00
Comments: Preprint: 25 pages
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Motivated by the human ability to integrate experience across language, perception, and action, we introduce UniWALM, a unified world-action-language model that learns video, action, and language in a single shared representation, where each modality is both a conditioning signal and a supervision target. We ask: can jointly generating language enrich this shared representation, improving manipulation and supporting memory-dependent behavior? Unlike prior approaches that attach a reasoning or vision-language model to a video-action backbone and run them as distinct stages, UniWALM unifies all three modalities inside a single mixture-of-transformers backbone whose per-modality diffusion heads co-denoise synchronously, decoding subtask and textual memory in the same pass as action with no separate planning stage; the generated language is re-consumed on the next step, closing a language-as-memory loop without an external planner or memory bank. To keep the action stream reliable under this richer objective, we further introduce Flow Matching with Correction (FMC), a training objective that teaches recovery from perturbed action trajectories at no inference-time cost. UniWALM consistently improves over same-backbone baselines (e.g., FastWAM) on compositional pick-and-place (MolmoSpaces) and across the memory-dependent subset of RMBench, where it reaches 34.5% without any pretrained language or reasoning model and exceeds Mem-0's keyframe-equipped setup (28.5%) using only a single anchor frame. On a real-world Find-the-Item task, generating textual memory lifts success from 11.1% to 61.1%, while state-of-the-art VLAs stay at or below chance (<=33.3%).

MindView: Benchmarking Generative Multi-View Spatial Intelligence
Authors: Yanbo Wang, Siqi Luo, Siyu Zhang, Chenming Zhu, Wenzhao Zheng, Qian Yu, Juncheng Yan, Bin Fu, Tai Wang, Yu Qiao, Yihao Liu, Jiwen Lu
First: 2026-10-20T17:58:25+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T17:58:25+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Spatial intelligence has recently been assessed through generative tasks such as spatially grounded image editing, yet language-guided generation of spatially consistent views from partial multi-view observations remains underexplored. We introduce MindView, a benchmark for generative multi-view spatial intelligence with 1,500 samples from 513 scenes across three indoor datasets. Its seven tasks form two families: geometry-dominant tasks specify changes in camera position and orientation, while semantics-dominant tasks require inferring target viewpoints by grounding referred objects and interpreting spatial relations and reference-frame cues. Evaluation of eleven generators reveals substantial room for improvement on MindView. A paired context analysis further shows that all eleven generators obtain lower overall scores under the expanded context setting. These findings motivate us to investigate whether task-specific supervision can improve target-view generation and whether models can use additional multi-view evidence effectively. We therefore fine-tune BAGEL on the MindView training dataset, improving its overall score by 24.3 points and yielding gains across all seven MindView tasks. Beyond these generation gains, the fine-tuned model also yields higher scores on three spatial-understanding benchmarks without training on their annotations, suggesting that learning to generate spatially grounded views may benefit spatial understanding. To improve evidence utilization under expanded context, we propose MindCue, a training-free spatial evidence selector guided by a target-view hypothesis. MindCue reverses the drop in overall score under expanded context and outperforms the standard-context baseline. Overall, MindView provides a concrete testbed for generative multi-view spatial intelligence.

Measuring, Localizing, and Ablating Alignment Signatures in LLMs
Authors: Aniket Anand, Janvijay Singh, Zhewei Sun, Dilek Hakkani-Tür, Nick Feamster
First: 2026-05-28T20:01:51+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T17:29:28+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Aligned language models often exhibit a recognizable AI-like style, yet its connection to post-training and internal representations remains poorly understood. In this work, we study whether post-training introduces or amplifies AI-like stylistic regularities and whether these regularities have a localized internal signature. To this end, we compare human text, base-model generations, and aligned-model generations under matched human-source prefixes. Aligned generations show lower human-corpus affinity and higher AI-detection rates than base generations, suggesting that post-training shifts generated text away from human-corpus style and toward detector-visible AI-like text. We then introduce PASTA (Post-training Alignment Signature Targeted Ablation), a training-free method that estimates a post-training alignment signature from aligned-base residual contrasts and ablates the corresponding direction during decoding. Across 11 aligned models and 6 AI detectors, PASTA lowers the detection rate for most aligned models; this effect transfers well across detectors and is not reproduced by random directions. Qualitative analysis suggests that PASTA generations remain relevant and coherent while exhibiting greater stylistic variation. Together, these results show that AI-like stylistic effects of post-training can be measured, localized, and causally tested through activation ablation.

Agentic Ensemble with Vision-Language Models for Visual Anomaly Detection
Authors: Shangquan Sun, Wenjun Luo, Xu He, Yoonsoo Lee, Shijian Lu
First: 2026-10-20T17:22:25+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T17:22:25+00:00

Abstract
Visual anomaly detection aims to detect abnormal objects, while anomaly samples are often scarce during model training. Assembling multiple pretrained detectors can help by leveraging their transferable image scores and anomaly maps. However, most existing ensemble methods apply fixed fusion rules over saved predictions which struggle when pretrained detectors clearly disagree due to different training objectives, priors, and visual representations. To this end, we design AnomalyAgent, a post-training fusion framework for accurate visual anomaly detection. AnomalyAgent aligns heterogeneous scores and maps, summarizes score- and region-level disagreement, and treats fusion as a finite action-selection problem. It implements two types of actions: 1) score actions that determine how to fuse image-level detector confidence via consensus, robust aggregation, detector weighting, or score-map consistency; 2) map actions that fuse dense anomaly responses via consensus, detector weighting, geometric aggregation, or disagreement correction. A constrained vision-language model (VLM) is introduced to inspect high-conflict samples and select valid score action and map action to produce the final prediction. We evaluate AnomalyAgent on MVTec AD and VisA as primary benchmarks, together with seven additional public anomaly benchmarks and Real-IAD for large-scale score-level evaluation. Across these benchmarks, AnomalyAgent consistently improves over equal-weight fusion and achieves the best mean image AUROC and pixel AP among the compared fusion methods on MVTec AD and VisA, while demonstrating robust transfer across additional anomaly domains.

Abstract
Collaborative perception enables agents to retrieve the scene semantics and geometry beyond the field of view of any individual agent. This broader scene understanding enhances transportation safety and improves efficiency in multi-robot systems. However, existing collaborative semantic occupancy methods rely on dense 3D voxel annotations and are limited to recognizing objects within a predefined set of semantic classes. To overcome these limitations, we present CoGaussian, an open-vocabulary Gaussian-based collaborative semantic occupancy prediction framework. CoGaussian represents a scene as a set of foundation model-aligned sparse 3D Gaussians, which serve as a compact representation for communicating and fusing geometric and semantic information across multiple agents. On the Semantic-OPV2V benchmark, our method outperforms existing open-vocabulary single-agent approaches, highlighting the effectiveness of open-vocabulary collaborative scene understanding.

GazeLens for Efficient Egocentric Video Question Answering with Simple Gaze-Driven Adaptive Token Reduction

Abstract
Smart glasses can help users understand their activities and surroundings, but small vision-language models must preserve useful evidence within limited computation. This is difficult because redundant frames consume the visual budget, while aggressive spatial compression can discard relevant details. Although gaze identifies the wearer's focus, the question may require evidence from the surrounding scene. To address these constraints, we propose GazeLens, which combines frame selection with gaze- and question-guided token reduction. First, HOG transitions and image sharpness select frames before visual encoding. For each selected frame, Gaussian pooling then forms gaze-focused and complementary summaries. Because their relevance depends on the question, a lightweight gate blends them into a primary token. Together with a global scene token, this retains both focused and contextual summaries within two tokens per frame. On StreamGaze, GazeLens achieves higher macro and micro accuracy than the evaluated full-grid and token-reduction baselines, with lower input-forward FLOPs than the shared-backbone token-reduction baselines.

CLOSER: Evidence-Aware Active Visual Verification for Embodied Object-Goal Navigation
Authors: Pengying Wu, Mingye Jiang, Yunze Hu, Zhejia Wen, Kangjie Zhou, Zongru Yang, Zhe Peng, Chang Liu
First: 2026-10-20T16:27:33+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T16:27:33+00:00
Comments: 13 pages, 8 figures, 10 tables
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Object-goal navigation (ObjectNav) requires an embodied agent to find an instance of a specified category in an unseen environment and stop near it. The agent typically relies on an object detector but must judge online from a limited egocentric video stream, sometimes a single frame, whether a detected candidate is the target. Detectors often confidently mistake similar objects, such as a sofa for a bed, and stopping at a false positive fails the episode. Prevailing systems also approach candidates greedily, leaving them occluded or partially observed. We present CLOSER, an evidence-aware active verification framework that treats stopping as closed-loop evidence acquisition, coupling vision-language model (VLM) verification with active perception. CLOSER fine-tunes an open-source VLM to label each view of a candidate tracked across frames as target, non-target, or uncertain, selects complementary views with an embedding-based diversity gate, and fuses their per-view probabilities with a lightweight network. If the fused judgment remains uncertain, a diffusion policy drives the agent toward the candidate using only the tracked mask and depth until the decision becomes decisive. CLOSER runs locally and plugs into existing ObjectNav systems without retraining them. On Gibson and Habitat-Matterport 3D, it raises average success rate by 3.6 points and success weighted by path length by 2.0 points, showing that verifying evidence before committing improves embodied navigation.

A Multi-Agent Framework for Automated Machine Learning with Evidence-based Planning for Medical Image Analysis

Abstract
Deep learning has become the default tool for medical image analysis, driven by the open-source movement. However, achieving the best performance on a given task remains difficult, with architecture selection and hyperparameter optimization (HPO) still considered "more of an art than a science". Automated machine learning (AutoML) automates parts of model training, yet helps little in medical imaging: general-purpose approaches lack medical architectures and preprocessing, and medical-focused ones demand engineering expertise and remain confined to narrow tasks such as 3D segmentation. We introduce MedicalMLOmni, a multi-agent AutoML framework that automates model training for diverse single- and multi-modal medical tasks through a natural language interface. Specifically, MedicalMLOmni performs evidence-based planning by retrieving published results to guide model selection and configuration, and employs parallel HPO where multiple agents explore adjustment strategies concurrently. MedicalMLOmni achieves the best score on 19 of 20 benchmarks; PMC-VQA is the exception, where a vision-language model with broader pre-training leads. Backbone-matched controls attribute the gains to the framework, not the backbone. By removing coding requirements, MedicalMLOmni lowers the barrier for domain experts with rich datasets but limited engineering expertise.

VisualEvolve: A Skill Self-Evolution Framework for Visual Reasoning
Authors: Jiawei Wang, Yucun Li, Xueqi Li, Shenghao Wang, Xinquan Gong, Dan Zhang, Zheqi Lv, Yuquan Le, Shaozu Yuan, An Zhang, Da Cao, Tat-Seng Chua
First: 2026-10-20T15:37:38+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T15:37:38+00:00
Comments: 29 pages, 9 figures. Project page: https://jiaweiwang-ai.github.io/VisualEvolve/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Visual tool use enables vision-language models (VLMs) to acquire task-relevant evidence, but its effectiveness can be limited by predefined visual operations and the tool-use strategies learned during training. Representing these operations and strategies as reusable external skills allows them to be refined through self-evolution without updating model parameters. However, skill refinement can introduce performance regressions. Meanwhile, inspecting accumulated task execution trajectories together with skill evolution history may lead to evolution evidence overload. To address these challenges, we propose VisualEvolve, a self-evolving framework that evolves executable visual operations and tool-use strategies for VLMs through a Select--Execute--Evolve--Evaluate cycle. Skill-Library Snapshot Selection uses performance on a fixed validation set disjoint from execution tasks to select an effective historical snapshot for subsequent evolution, limiting the propagation of performance regressions. Progressive Evidence Access (PEA) guides capability-gap diagnosis through a coarse-to-fine hierarchy comprising the Skill-Performance Report, Evolution Log, Trajectory Summaries, and relevant raw trajectory segments. Across four VLM backbones and six evaluation sets from three benchmarks, VisualEvolve improves over the Initial Skill Library in 23 of 24 settings, with a maximum gain of 15.1 percentage points on VisualProbe Hard using Qwen3.5-Plus. Ablation studies support the contributions of Snapshot Selection and PEA, while further experiments show that skills can be improved using experience from different data settings and directly reused across backbones. Moreover, VisualEvolve enables effective skill self-evolution with over an order of magnitude fewer task-solving trajectories than estimated for representative RL-based methods.

CounterCount: Diagnosing Knowledge Bias in How Vision-Language Models Count Attributes
Authors: Reem Alzahrani, Bushra Bin Hemid, Hassan Alshanqiti, Zaid Alyafeai, Paul Gavrikov, Bernard Ghanem, Abdelrahman Eldesokey
First: 2026-05-18T04:00:05+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T15:34:58+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Do Vision-Language Models (VLMs) count what they see, or what they know about the world? We introduce CounterCount, a diagnostic dataset of paired factual and counterfactual (CF) images, in which the CF image contains an atypical count of a subject's attribute (e.g., a rabbit with four ears), together with annotations localizing the counted attributes. Humans answer both types of images nearly perfectly ($98.9\%$ and $97.5\%$), but across 16 VLMs (14 open-weight and 2 closed-source), average accuracy drops from $91.6\%$ on factual to $49.5\%$ on CF images, with most errors stemming from responses that match the canonical count, therefore revealing a bias towards knowledge priors rather than reliance on visual information. Based on these findings, we perform an analysis that reveals two interesting insights behind this gap. First, models exhibit a ``System-1''-like response behavior, falling back on canonical counts after a shallow recognition of the subject. Second, model activations partially contain the visual evidence necessary to answer correctly, yet models underutilize it, attending less to the counted attributes when they provide biased answers. Input manipulations that break visual context, introduce cueing or reasoning, or direct amplification of the model's internal attention to relevant regions can reduce such biases. CounterCount goes beyond showing that VLMs fall back on knowledge priors, revealing that once a familiar context is recognized, models favor prior knowledge over available visual evidence rather than failing to encode that evidence.

Do Vision-Language Models (VLMs) count what they see, or what they know about the world?
Don't Skip the Baseline: A Controlled Comparison of Dynamic Expert Skipping in MoEs
Authors: Jakob Steimle, Thomas Elsken, Daniel Ehrhardt, Lukas Rinder, Daniel Cremers, Xi Wang
First: 2026-10-20T15:29:19+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T15:29:19+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
A growing body of work proposes dynamic expert-skipping methods for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models that adjust the number of experts activated per token during inference, aiming to preserve model quality while further reducing the number of active parameters. These methods have added calibrated importance scores, inter-expert similarity metrics, or learned per-token allocators in order to identify experts to skip. However, different calibration regimes, different reductions in the effective number of experts, and evaluation suites make comparisons difficult. We classify four representative prior methods into a unified taxonomy and benchmark them against a natural, simple baseline: statically reducing the number of experts $k$ (i.e., same reduced $k$ across all layers and tokens). We provide a fair comparison of these methods at matched expert-compute budget, on two modern MoEs (Gemma4-26B-A4B and Qwen3-30B-A3B) in a text-only setting and a contemporary vision-language model (Qwen3-VL) in a multimodal setting. Surprisingly, we find that the simple baseline is hard to outperform consistently across models, modalities, benchmarks, and calibration settings. The methods we benchmark often underperform the baseline and achieved gains, when present, are minor. We further find that the effective $k$ achieved (or optimized for) in calibration can diverge from the effective $k$ in testing, resulting in over- or under-estimating the compute at inference time. Based on these findings, we recommend a calibration-free per-modality static $k$ reduction and validate this on a held-out model and modality. We further argue that a static control at matched measured compute is the baseline any dynamic skipping method must beat.

Abstract
Realistic few-shot adaptation of medical vision-language models (VLMs) can leave low-prevalence classes without any labeled support. The training-free SS- Text+ solver remains operational in this setting, but its zero-support prototypes receive no visual adaptation and fall back to a purely textual class prototype. We in- troduce Gated Knowledge-Guided Prototype Transfer (GProtoT), a pre-repulsion module that transfers supported-class visual residuals to missing classes using VLM similarity and an ontology-based clinical distance. A validation-free evidence gate scales transfer based on semantic relevance and residual agreement, thereby limiting unsupported extrapolation. In 50 paired realistic one-shot trials on MESSIDOR with FLAIR features, severe and proliferative diabetic retinopathy were absent from every support set. Relative to SS-Text+, gated ProtoT improved missing-class recall by 5.23 points, average class accuracy by 1.37 points, and the missing/supported recall harmonic mean by 1.83 points. It reduced negative- transfer frequency from 46-48% for ungated transfer to 2%, while at the cost of reductions of 1.20 percentage points in supported-class recall and 0.45 percentage points in top-1 accuracy. These results indicate that evidence-based gating provides a safety mechanism for the knowledge source. GProtoT is based on SS- Text+ and requires no gradients, validation data, or additional model inference, of- fering a low-compute path toward accountable adaptation under incomplete clini- cal supervision. Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have recently emerged as a promising paradigm for end-to-end autonomous driving by enabling unified reasoning across perception, language, and planning. However, existing approaches lack mechanisms to exploit past failures or adapt to distribution shifts, causing the model to persistently underperform on similar scenarios where it has previously failed. In this paper, we propose DriveVLA-M0, a retrieval-augmented VLA with failure-aware latent memory. We construct a latent memory pool that stores failure cases along with their structure scene representations and expert trajectory labels, and design a dedicated Retrieve Model that decouples static road structure and dynamic agent interactions to enable structurally grounded retrieval. PACE: Plan, Attend, Check, and Emit for Training-Free Response Timing in Streaming Video Understanding
Authors: Siqi Guo, Yuxia Chen, Wenbo Zhu, Yangguang Ji, Zhenxiang Jiang, Teng Zhong, Yu Zhang
First: 2026-10-20T14:58:18+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T14:58:18+00:00
Comments: Accepted at MMM 2027
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Streaming video understanding requires a model to decide not only what to answer but also when to answer. Evidence arrives progressively, and recent benchmarks identify premature responses, issued before the queried event has concluded, as a recurring failure mode. Existing approaches often learn response timing through additional training, while training-free alternatives typically rely on scalar gating signals that do not explicitly represent when an answer becomes final. We organize chunk-wise streaming errors into three patterns: sampling variation can make an answer unstable, a stable answer may lack support in the observed video, or an answer may be supported by the current evidence while the event remains unfinished. We propose PACE (Plan, Attend, Check, and Emit), a training-free framework designed around these three failure modes. Given a question, PACE drafts a plan specifying what to observe and what will signal completion. As the stream unfolds, it invokes an off-the-shelf video LLM at informative moments, evaluates each draft for stability, grounding in its cited frames, and completion, and records verified observations in an event ledger. Experiments on ProactiveVideoQA and OVO-Bench Forward show that PACE improves over the official protocols by up to 5.9 average PAUC points and 6.2 average accuracy points across proprietary and open-source backbones, yielding state-of-the-art performance.

ABACUS: A Benchmark for Image Editing Models to Assess Object Category and Counting Under Scene Conservation
Authors: Aymen Lassoued, Angelique Loesch, Romaric Audigier
Venue: WACV 2027
First: 2026-10-20T14:53:22+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T14:53:22+00:00
Comments: Accepted at WACV 2027 (Evaluations & Datasets track). Code and data: https://github.com/CEA-LIST/ABACUS
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Instruction-based image editing models can now add, remove a specified number of objects or change their category while preserving the scene. Yet these models often fail to produce the correct number, and these failures grow sharply as scenes become more complex and the requested count increases. Current benchmarks largely miss this problem: they score edits with holistic similarity metrics or with vision-language model (VLM) judges that are themselves poor at counting, so numerical errors go unnoticed or are graded incorrectly. As a result, the true numerical ability of today's editing models has never been accurately measured. We address this issue with ABACUS, a new benchmark, evaluation protocol and dedicated scoring metric for numerical image editing. Our protocol is judge-free: no VLM or MLLM scores an edit or counts objects (a VLM only confirms the category of rare ambiguous crops). Instead, it verifies each edit at the instance level against panoptic ground truth confirmed by detector consensus, checking both that the exact requested count was achieved and that the rest of the scene was preserved, across the add, remove and change operations. By evaluating leading image editors, our method reveals substantial numerical editing failures that worsen with increasing scene complexity and object count; failures that prior benchmarks often overlooked or misjudged.

Abstract
Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) enables assessment of cardiac anatomy, ventricular function, and myocardial tissue characteristics. Clinicians interpret these images by identifying cardiac structures and focusing on the regions relevant to each clinical question, motivating anatomically guided vision-language models (VLMs). Yet CMR-specific supervision for anatomical localisation and clinical question answering remains limited. To address this gap, we investigate fine-grained CMR visual question answering through anatomical grounding and guided attention. We construct 128,915 anatomical-grounding and 42,799 clinical QA pairs across short-axis cine, late gadolinium enhancement, and long-axis cine. Combining anatomical grounding pretraining with CARA yields our model, CARA-VL. Together, our data and method provide a practical framework for studying and advancing cardiac visual understanding in VLMs. We will release the QA data derived from public datasets upon publication.

Adaptive Replay Refinement: Allocating Test-Time Compute Between Revision and Resampling
Authors: Ahsan Bilal, Muhammad Ahmed Mohsin, Muhammad Umer, Ayesha Mohsin, Sarmad Bilal, Dean F. Hougen
First: 2026-10-20T14:41:46+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-20T14:41:46+00:00

Abstract
Test-time scaling allocates additional inference compute to refining existing solutions or generating new ones. This raises a fundamental question: Should I keep improving this solution, or stop working on it and try a completely new solution? Existing methods make this decision using fixed refinement schedules, self-verification judgments, or learned controllers, which can waste compute or rely on signals that may be incorrect or require additional training. We introduce Adaptive Replay Refinement (ARR), a training-free method that detects when a stationary revision reaches a fixed point and reallocates the released compute to a new independent sample; replay decoding treats the current solution as a draft and verifies unchanged tokens in parallel, enabling efficient fixed-point detection. Across seven models from five families and seven benchmarks, ARR matches majority voting on three of five mathematical reasoning benchmarks at equal compute, while reducing critical-path serial passes by up to 2.4 times. On MATH500, ARR achieves 84.6% accuracy with only 41% of the serial passes required by majority voting. These results demonstrate that detecting refinement exhaustion enables adaptive reallocation from revision to independent sampling, improving inference efficiency while highlighting the limits of self-revision. Code is available at https://github.com/AhsanBilal7/adaptive_refine.

Authors: Md Anwar Hossen, Nathan R. Tallent, Luanzheng Guo, Ali Jannesary

Abstract
Scientific discovery increasingly relies on federated learning over data from high-resolution instruments, and these data are strongly non-IID and long-tailed. Coreset selection could shorten training, but existing federated coreset methods either prune with local heuristics that ignore the global class distribution, or first train a warm-up model at every client and upload class centroids of its features. This makes coreset selection a distributed bottleneck that consumes GPU time at every client, grows with the model and the number of clients, and must be repeated for every architecture. Each client scores its samples once with a frozen, compact vision-language model and uploads scalar statistics per class. The server merges these into a global class profile, which lets each client remove anomalies and prune redundant samples only from classes that are locally abundant but globally common. As the number of clients grows from 10 to 500, SCOPE's time before training grows by fewer seconds than any baseline's, and at 500 clients the baselines need 1.7 to 3.6x SCOPE's time. Abstract
Existing feature lifting approaches for open-vocabulary segmentation rely on simple backprojection to assign features to primitives. In the case of 3D Gaussian splatting reconstruction, the primitives overlap heavily, leading to suboptimal reconstructions, governed by many primitives per ray. Backprojection, however, reduces to a least-squares solvable problem, exactly when no two primitives share a ray. Abstract

Abstract

Abstract

Abstract
Large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are successful in addressing a multitude of vision-language understanding tasks, such as Visual Question Answering (VQA), but their memory and compute requirements remain a concern for practical deployment. A promising class of techniques for mitigating this concern is Knowledge Distillation, where knowledge from a high-capacity Teacher network is transferred to a considerably smaller Student network. However, the capacity gap between the two networks is both a blessing and a curse: the smaller the Student network, the better its efficiency, and the stronger (typically, also larger) the Teacher, the more knowledge it carries; yet, beyond a point, the larger capacity gap between the two leads to worse knowledge transfer. Abstract
Class-Incremental Learning (CIL) enables models to learn new classes continually while preserving past knowledge. Recently, vision-language models like CLIP offer transferable features via multimodal pre-training, making them well-suited for CIL.
