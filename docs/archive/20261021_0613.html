How Well Do Vision-Language Models Understand Movies?

Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) can increasingly handle extended contexts and answer questions about long videos--but can they understand the narrative structure of movies as humans do? To investigate this question, we introduce MF$^2$ (Movie Facts and Fibs), a fully manually curated benchmark designed to evaluate whether models can identify, recall, and comprehend information central to the movie's storyline, using both the visual and textual modalities. MF$^2$ includes over 50 open-licensed movies, each paired with manually constructed sets of claim pairs: one true claim (fact) and one plausible but false (fib), totaling over 850 pairs. Our analysis across 20 open-source and 2 proprietary models reveals a substantial gap relative to human performance. While subtitles provide a strong signal for narrative understanding, other textual sources (e.g., synopses) are less informative. In contrast, when visual information is distributed across the entire movie and jointly embedded with subtitles, models struggle to identify and effectively use the relevant content. However, when the visual input is restricted to claim-relevant visual content, models improve significantly, indicating substantial room for improvement in processing large multimodal contexts and distinguishing relevant from irrelevant information. Overall, our findings highlight persistent challenges in multimodal narrative understanding for current VLMs. We release our data and code to support further research in this direction.

SpecKCast: Spectral Kalman Forecasting for Diffusion Acceleration
Authors: Javad Rajabi, Hakki Can Karaimer, David B. Lindell, Babak Taati, Michael S. Brown
First: 2026-10-19T17:58:04+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-19T17:58:04+00:00
Comments: 34 pages, 19 figures
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) achieve high-fidelity image and video generation, but they suffer from substantial inference costs due to repeated transformer evaluations across many denoising steps. Feature caching reduces this cost by reusing features across denoising steps. Recent forecasting methods extend this idea by extrapolating features to skipped steps instead of reusing them. However, existing forecasting methods extrapolate the features they predict uniformly, even though the spatial-frequency components of these features change most at different stages of denoising. They also overlook that denoising progresses nonuniformly, so equal step intervals can correspond to very different amounts of change. These mismatches amplify forecast errors as more steps are skipped, limiting the achievable acceleration. We propose SpecKCast, a training-free method that forecasts the model output in the frequency domain. SpecKCast applies a 2D discrete cosine transform (DCT) to the output and tracks each coefficient with a lightweight Kalman filter along a band-specific time coordinate derived from the noise schedule. The filter tracks each coefficient's value and rate of change, and the transformer is evaluated only when the filter's forecast uncertainty exceeds a threshold. Experiments on FLUX.1-dev, Qwen-Image, Wan2.1-14B, and HunyuanVideo show that SpecKCast achieves a better trade-off between acceleration and fidelity than existing caching and forecasting methods, reaching up to 6x speedup with less than 0.05 GiB of additional memory.

Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) show promise for agricultural diagnosis but fail unpredictably in zero-shot settings. We test whether this stems from poor visual features or an inability to verbalize what the model already perceives, using a benchmark spanning classification and detection drawn from 118 AgML classification and 54 detection datasets. Linear probing shows agricultural features are linearly separable in frozen VLM vision towers, with Gemma-4-31B's encoder reaching F1-macro of 0.60--0.78 across tasks and approaching the vision-only DINOv3 backbone, indicating the bottleneck lies downstream of perception. We then test whether test-time scaling, an iterative in-context skill-memory loop, can close this gap without updating weights. Gains are modest and inconsistent. Skill-memory improves classification accuracy by up to 3.1 points on three of four tasks while regressing on species, with gains concentrated on classes seen during optimization and unseen classes regressing. Detection F1 is effectively unchanged (0.412 to 0.421), though an oracle filtering condition reaches 0.432 F1, revealing real headroom unconstrained prompting fails to surface. These results indicate VLMs see more agricultural features than they report, but test-time scaling alone fails to surface that hidden parametric knowledge, motivating agentic approaches grounded in external, retrievable knowledge.

What Language Adds, and at What Cost: Four Controlled Probes of Pathology Vision-Language Models
Authors: Wenhao Zhang, Zhongliang Zhou, Yi Ping, Zirui Wang, Sheng Li
First: 2026-10-19T17:48:31+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-19T17:48:31+00:00

Abstract
Vision--language models (VLMs) promise to make computational pathology queryable and able to draft reports, but how much language adds beyond the vision backbone is unclear, because almost every system is evaluated by its developers on test sets of their own choosing. We grade published evidence by how it was produced and test four claims with controlled probes that reuse released checkpoints and public data, varying one input at a time. Report text adds prognostic signal: adding a frozen embedding of the stage-redacted report to a slide embedding raises the pooled C-index by 0.019 for progression-free interval and by 0.016 for overall survival across 1{,}633 TCGA patients, and restoring the stage statement adds nothing. Prompts add no measurable accuracy at inference: with features and pooling fixed, zero-shot classification is statistically indistinguishable from a supervised linear probe for lung and renal cancer subtyping. Slide-level question answering is barely grounded: SlideChat answers 74.82\% of SlideBench-VQA(TCGA) items correctly with the slide and 74.41\% without it, whereas its language backbone alone reaches 43.52\%. Paraphrasing leaves accuracy unchanged but flips 8--23\% of answers. Self-reported gains from agentic navigation and few-shot language priors cannot yet be attributed to language. Language thus adds real but narrow value, and we propose minimum controls that would expose these failure modes in future evaluations.

Just Initialize: A Training-Free Initialization Component for Large-Scale Routing Optimization
Authors: Jiale Zhao, Sirui Mao, Zimu Chen, Wentao Yang, Zihan Wang, Xuefeng Huang, Junji Cheng, Liyuanjun Lai

Abstract
Large-scale routing problems are difficult to solve efficiently as their search spaces grow rapidly with problem size. Existing approaches primarily improve the optimization procedure itself, often at increasing computational cost. We instead shift the focus to a useful initialization that can be refined into a high-quality solution with limited downstream refinement. We propose Just Initialize, a training-free and solver-agnostic initialization component for large-scale routing optimization. Just Initialize compresses a large routing instance into a compact surrogate space, optimizes its global routing structure, and recovers the resulting solution as an optimization-friendly starting point in the original space. Extensive experiments on Traveling Salesman Problems (TSPs), Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problems (CVRPs), Vehicle Routing Problems with Time Windows (VRPTWs), and Prize-Collecting Traveling Salesman Problems (PCTSPs) demonstrate that Just Initialize achieves high-quality solutions comparable to or better than state-of-the-art methods while substantially reducing computational cost across instances ranging from 1K to 100K nodes, including an average speedup of approximately 70$\times$, sub-second runtimes on 10K-node instances, and runtimes within tens of seconds on 100K-node instances.

Learning to Think and Explore in 3D World
Authors: Yijiang Li, Bingyang Wang, Nuno Vasconcelos

Comments: Project page: https://williamium3000.github.io/act3d/

Abstract
We study how vision-language models (VLMs) can learn to think and actively explore in 3D space. Instead of providing multi-view images, videos, or 3D representations to the model, we preserve the native visual encoding of VLMs, i.e., a single image, one at a time, just like humans, and treat the 3D representation only as an environment with which the model can interact through actions (e.g., move left) and observations (rendered images). Each rollout begins from an initial first-person view and a visual question. The model can then iteratively reason, navigate, and reorient its viewpoint before committing to a final answer. Following an initial trajectory-supervised finetuning, we apply reinforcement learning (RL) with rewards driven primarily by final-answer correctness. Crucially, RL provides no direct supervision over which viewpoints the model should visit or which exploration actions it should take. Instead, the model must learn an effective exploration policy solely from the extent to which its interactions improve performance. Across six 3D benchmarks, our model ACT3D achieves an average accuracy of 53.2% that improves its base Qwen model by 22.0% and surpasses proprietary Gemini 3.6 Flash. ACT3D also outperforms existing open-source 3D methods while using 50-70% fewer input frames, and generalizes to unseen domains with an average improvement of 5.9% over its supervised initialization. Further analysis reveals adaptive, question-conditioned exploration. Performance gains over the base model increase from 0.6% on questions answerable from the initial view to 16.3% on those requiring extensive exploration. ACT3D searches for occluded targets, avoids unnecessary exploration, and acquires complementary viewpoints for spatial comparisons. These findings demonstrate that outcome-based RL enables VLMs not only to reason over visual evidence, but also to learn when and how to acquire it.

We study how vision-language models (VLMs) can learn to think and actively explore in 3D space.
Visual Text Compression as Measure Transport
Authors: Lv Tang, Tianyi Zheng, Yang Liu, Bo Li, Xingyu Li
First: 2026-05-06T19:02:27+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-19T17:11:03+00:00
Comments: This paper is accpeted by NeurIPS2026
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Visual text compression (VTC) promises efficient long-context processing by rendering text into an image and re-encoding it with a vision-language model, often producing $3$--$20\times$ fewer decoder tokens than subword tokenization. Yet token savings do not translate predictably into downstream utility: on some tasks the visual path matches or exceeds the text path, on others it collapses, and the compression ratio itself does not predict which regime will occur. The missing quantity is therefore not another summary of efficiency, but a principled measure of task-relevant information loss induced by visual encoding. We address this problem by formulating VTC in the language of measure transport. Treating text and visual tokens as empirical probability measures, we show that the ViT patch encoder induces a push-forward map whose transport cost decomposes into a precision cost from within-patch aggregation and a coverage cost from cross-patch fragmentation. Both terms are estimable from downstream-label-free probes. This formulation yields two operational consequences: a downstream-label-free routing criterion that selects whether to use the visual path for a given input or benchmark instance, and a transport-informed foveation mechanism that re-encodes high-cost regions at higher resolution. Across $24$ NLP datasets at Qwen3-4B, our label-free rule matches the per-dataset oracle on $17/24$ datasets ($70.8\%$), and improves the average task score by $+3.3\%$ with $-10.3\%$ average tokens relative to a pure-LLM.

CAVA: Complementary-Label-Aware Data Valuation
Authors: Tan-Ha Mai, Hsuan-Tien Lin, Vu Nguyen
First: 2026-10-19T17:00:37+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-19T17:00:37+00:00
Comments: 39 pages, 18 figures
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Complementary-label learning (CLL) trains a classifier from labels that name a class an instance does not belong to. Such labels are cheap to collect from crowdworkers or vision-language models (VLMs), making them attractive for large-scale annotation. However, both sources can mistakenly assign the true class as the complementary label. Such annotation errors are particularly harmful in CLL because the model is explicitly trained to suppress the correct answer. Optimal transport (OT) data valuation identifies harmful samples by comparing training data with a clean validation set, but transferring it directly to CLL creates a semantic mismatch: label $c$ means not class $c$ in the training but class $c$ in the validation. We show that this makes the OT label groups noisy mixtures whose behavior depends on the noise rate. The transferred score is informative only at sufficiently high noise, becomes uninformative at the crossover rate $1/M$ for $M$ classes, and reverses below it, where practical CLL annotation typically operates. We propose CAVA, which introduces a complementary label cost and a label margin that isolates the contribution of each complementary label to the transport potential. The margin keeps a consistent direction across noise rates, detects diagonal noise with a parameter-free threshold of closed-form precision, and allows detected samples to be recycled as ordinary-label supervision. On synthetic and human-annotated benchmarks, CAVA separates diagonal noise almost perfectly where directly transferred OT valuation performs worse than random. With only a fraction of the clean validation data, it also improves every CLL objective we evaluate, showing that OT-based valuation becomes reliable once the semantics of complementary supervision are modeled explicitly.

Complementary-label learning (CLL) trains a classifier from labels that name a class an instance does not belong to.

Abstract
Composed Image Retrieval (CIR) retrieves products matching a reference image modified by natural language---a core capability for eCommerce search. Existing training-free methods fuse CLIP embeddings via arithmetic, but we identify a fundamental limitation: user text modifications are attribute-sparse---a user who types ``make it red with shorter sleeves'' specifies what to change but not what to keep (the floral pattern, the V-neck, the fit-and-flare silhouette). We introduce language-space fusion: a Vision-Language Model (VLM) jointly interprets the reference image and modification to generate a visually grounded product-title caption---e.g., ``Red floral fit-and-flare dress with short sleeves and V-neck''---that fills in the missing attributes from the image. Our method, CaptionFuse, requires no task-specific training and is model-agnostic. A controlled experiment confirms that attribute content--- not linguistic style---drives the gains: imperative and descriptive captions with identical attributes achieve equal R@10, while both outperform raw modifications by +6.5 points.

Abstract
Spatial intelligence requires agents to actively acquire visual evidence and integrate observations into a coherent representation of the three-dimensional world. Yet spatial question answering offers limited evidence of whether models can construct accurate metric maps through autonomous exploration. We introduce SpatialScout, a benchmark for evaluating vision-language model (VLM) agents on active object-level 3D mapping. SpatialScout comprises 80 diverse indoor scenes containing 3,140 target objects. Given an object inventory, RGB observations, camera calibration, and camera poses, agents control a camera to explore each scene and estimate object centers in a shared coordinate frame. The task requires coordinating viewpoint selection, cross-view association, geometric estimation, and map revision under bounded interaction budgets. Evaluation jointly measures localization accuracy and target coverage through object-scale overlap scores that penalize missing predictions. Experiments with frontier VLMs reveal substantial limitations: the strongest evaluated model, Claude Opus 5.5, achieves a mean overlap score of 41.71%, with 47.23% of targets exceeding an intersection-over-union (IoU) threshold of 0.5 and only 3.25% exceeding 0.9. These results reveal a substantial gap between recovering coarse object layouts and constructing complete, precise metric maps. SpatialScout provides a quantitative testbed for developing agents that build reliable 3D spatial representations through active perception. Abstract
Can the robot use a plate to cut a cake if no knife is available? Tool use greatly expands robot capabilities, but to use tools creatively beyond their intended functions, the robot faces the challenge of $\textit{open-world affordance grounding}$: select an open-category object to act as a tool and localize its specific region of action. ScopeMem: Latent Memory That Learns Where to Revise Frozen GUI Agents

Abstract
Latent memory is gaining ground as a lightweight history supplement for GUI agents. It supplies history to a frozen vision-language model via prefix vectors trained with step-wise cross-entropy (CE). While resulting gains are naturally attributed to encoded history, our controlled experiments show they primarily stem from action revision. The prefix learns to alter the model's default actions. However, this revision is unscoped because step-wise CE pushes outputs toward reference actions at every step without explicitly preserving correct decisions. Consequently, it repairs errors but also breaks 8-13% of initially correct answers. Overall accuracy metrics hide these losses. We therefore propose ScopeMem, a latent-memory method for scoped revision. Its encoder conditions prefix generation on the model's initial answer and is trained to correct errors while preserving correct decisions. On the controlled ablation set, ScopeMem reduces the share of broken correct decisions to 5.6% while repairing more errors than any evaluated CE-trained prefix. On AndroidControl, GUI-Odyssey, and zero-shot AITW, ScopeMem outperforms all evaluated prompting and latent-memory baselines, enabling a frozen Qwen3-VL-8B to match or surpass GUI-specialized agents and Qwen3-VL-32B.

Abstract
We propose using Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for macro placement in chip floorplanning, a complex optimization task that has recently shown promising advancements through machine learning methods. Attention Sinks in VLMs Are Borrowed, Not Seen
Authors: Dengjia Zhang, Alexander Martin, Pengyu Yan, Xihan Sun, Jiaqi Li, Benjamin Van Durme, Andrew Yates, Reno Kriz, William Walden
First: 2026-10-11T19:20:41+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-19T14:19:24+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
We study how to detect and mitigate visual attention sinks in vision-language models (VLMs). Visual attention sinks are image tokens that absorb a large share of attention over the image regardless of the query or task, and they mislead methods that treat attention maps as a record of where the model ``looked.'' Prior work has detected sinks through massive activation dimensions and proposed redistributing their attention, but none have addressed what makes a token become a sink. Comparing six candidate detectors on ten VLMs from four families, we find that only the centered cosine similarity between an image token's key and the key of the language model's text-sink detects visual sinks. Thus, a visual sink is an image token whose key aligns with the text-sink's key. Moving a sink's key component along this direction to the median of the image keys sends its attention back to the text-sink (\textbf{Are Borrowed}) while leaving model answers unchanged~(\textbf{Not Seen}). Building on this, we propose a training-free, entropy-triggered re-look that first cleans the attention map, then crops the most-attended region of the cleaned map and inserts it into the reasoning trace. Cleaning adds negligible computation to the forward pass, and the re-look improves accuracy with a mean gain of $+4.3$ points on fine-detail VQA benchmarks across model families.

Abstract
Test-time scaling can improve LLM reasoning, but existing approaches typically adapt generation or apply answer selection after a predetermined generation schedule. We study within-instance allocation: under a hard per-instance prompt-plus-completion token cap, when should inference stop, generate another candidate, or spend residual tokens adjudicating candidates already observed? BOSS is a training-free policy for this three-way decision, with worst-case budget checks before every generation and judge call. Approximately matched-cost controls further characterize how the benefit of adjudication changes with available inference budget. Together, these results establish generation-adjudication allocation as a distinct design axis for reasoning under strict per-instance caps.

Dissecting Training-Free Uncertainty Estimation in Multimodal Large Language Models
Authors: Soroush Seifi, Vaggelis Dorovatas, Lin Li, Yarin Gal, Rahaf Aljundi
First: 2026-09-01T12:32:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-19T13:28:47+00:00

Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are increasingly deployed in safety-critical settings, yet estimating when their answers can be trusted remains underexplored. We systematically compare training-free verbalized, token-level and semantic uncertainty estimation methods on twelve MLLMs and five VQA benchmarks. We find that answer length decides which family wins: token-level entropy on short answers, verbalized methods on sentence-length responses, and semantic methods on long-form generation. Token-level entropy becomes far more informative when estimated at a higher temperature than the low ones used to generate answers, and asking a model to judge its own answer becomes more reliable with model size. Building on this, a length-aware recipe that additionally flags responses on which the model abstains outperforms every individual method on average at about a quarter of the cost of semantic methods, although no method attains the coverage required for safe deployment. These results provide practitioners with principled guidance for selecting estimators by answer length and model scale.

Abstract
Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) provide interpretability by routing predictions through a layer of human-understandable concepts.
